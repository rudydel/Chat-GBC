The Game Boy was announced by Nintendo in 1989 and went on sale in Japan on April 21, 1989 for 12,500 yen. The North American launch followed on July 31, 1989 at a price of 89.99 dollars, and Europe received the console on September 28, 1990. The Japanese launch line-up consisted of Super Mario Land, Alleyway, Baseball and Yakuman. In North America the console was bundled with Tetris, a decision pushed by Henk Rogers, who convinced Nintendo of America's Minoru Arakawa that a simple puzzle game everybody could play would sell more consoles than a Mario game aimed at children.

Tetris was designed by Alexey Pajitnov in Moscow in 1984. The Game Boy version, released in 1989, sold more than 35 million copies including the bundled cartridges and is often credited with the early success of the console. The Game Boy Tetris cartridge supports two player matches over the link cable, one of the first uses of the feature.

The console was designed by Nintendo Research and Development 1, the team led by Gunpei Yokoi, with Satoru Okada as the lead hardware engineer. Yokoi had joined Nintendo in 1965 as a maintenance engineer, created the Ultra Hand toy in 1970 and the Game and Watch series in 1980, and produced Metroid and Kid Icarus. His design philosophy, lateral thinking with withered technology, meant using cheap, mature and well understood components in new ways rather than chasing the newest parts. The Game Boy applied that philosophy: a slow 8 bit processor, a monochrome screen without a backlight and no colour, but a long battery life, a low price and a rugged case.

The processor is a Sharp LR35902, a custom chip whose core is called the SM83. Its instruction set resembles the Intel 8080 with some of the bit manipulation instructions of the Zilog Z80, but it lacks the Z80's index registers, the second register set and the io instructions, and it has no multiply or divide instruction. The chip runs at 4.194304 megahertz; the Game Boy Color can switch to 8.388608 megahertz. The same chip also contains the picture processing unit, the sound generator, the timers, the serial port and the joypad register, so the console needs very few other components.

The Game Boy has 8 kilobytes of work RAM and 8 kilobytes of video RAM. The screen is 160 pixels wide and 144 pixels high and refreshes 59.7 times a second. Graphics are made of 8 by 8 pixel tiles with 2 bits per pixel, giving four shades. The background is a scrolling map of 32 by 32 tiles, a window layer can cover part of the screen, and there are 40 sprites of 8 by 8 or 8 by 16 pixels, with at most 10 sprites on one scanline. The palette registers BGP, OBP0 and OBP1 map the four shades. A game can change the scroll registers during the frame to create effects such as parallax and wobbling water.

The sound hardware has four channels: two pulse wave channels, one of which has a frequency sweep, a programmable wave channel that plays 32 four bit samples, and a noise channel. Audio is mono through the internal speaker and stereo through the headphone jack. The volume wheel on the right side controls the speaker and the headphones.

Cartridges hold the game in read only memory. A cartridge of 32 kilobytes needs no extra chip; larger games use a memory bank controller to switch 16 kilobyte banks of ROM into the address space. The MBC1 supports up to 2 megabytes of ROM and 32 kilobytes of RAM, the MBC2 has 512 half bytes of built in RAM, the MBC3 adds a real time clock used by Pokemon Gold and Silver, and the MBC5 supports up to 8 megabytes of ROM and 128 kilobytes of RAM and works at the double speed of the Game Boy Color. Save games are kept in cartridge RAM powered by a small coin cell battery. When the console starts, its boot ROM scrolls the Nintendo logo down the screen and checks that the cartridge header contains the same logo and a correct checksum; otherwise the console halts.

The original Game Boy runs on four AA batteries for roughly 15 to 30 hours, far longer than the colour handhelds it competed against. A DC jack accepts an external power supply, and a battery pack accessory was sold as well. The console measures about 90 by 148 by 32 millimetres and weighs about 220 grams without batteries. The case is grey with a maroon and blue button colour scheme; the buttons are a directional pad, A, B, Start and Select, a layout that came from the NES controller. The contrast wheel on the left side adjusts the display, and a link cable port on the side connects two consoles for multiplayer games and trading.

The screen of the original model is a reflective super twisted nematic liquid crystal display with a green tint that is often described as pea soup. It shows four shades of greenish grey. Because the liquid crystals respond slowly, fast moving objects leave ghost trails, and some games flicker sprites on and off on purpose to create a fifth transparent shade. The display has no backlight, and third party accessories such as the Light Boy clipped a light and a magnifying lens onto the console.

The Game Boy Pocket came out in 1996. It is about 30 percent smaller and lighter, runs on two AAA batteries for about 10 hours, and has a true black and white screen with much less ghosting. It was sold in several colours, including red, yellow, green, blue, black, silver and clear. The Game Boy Light, sold only in Japan in 1998, added an electroluminescent backlight with a switch and ran on two AA batteries. Nintendo also sold the Play It Loud series in 1995, the original Game Boy in red, yellow, green, blue, black, white and clear cases.

The Game Boy Color was released in Japan on October 21, 1998 and in North America in November 1998. It has a colour screen that shows 56 colours at once from a palette of 32,768, a processor that can run twice as fast, 32 kilobytes of work RAM, 16 kilobytes of video RAM and an infrared port for wireless data transfer between two consoles. It is backwards compatible with all Game Boy games and colours them with one of several built in palettes chosen by holding a direction and a button while the logo appears. Colour only games have a black cartridge, dual mode games have a clear cartridge. The Game Boy Color runs on two AA batteries for about 10 hours.

The Super Game Boy, released in 1994, is a Super Nintendo cartridge that plays Game Boy games on a television with selectable colour palettes and screen borders. Some games contain special palettes and borders for it. The Game Boy Camera of 1998 is a cartridge with a swivelling camera that takes 128 by 112 pixel photographs in four shades, and the Game Boy Printer prints them on thermal paper. The Game Boy Camera was for a time listed as the smallest digital camera in the world.

Pokemon Red and Green were released in Japan on February 27, 1996 for the Game Boy and became the best selling games on the system, with Pokemon Red and Blue reaching North America in September 1998 and Pokemon Yellow following. Players trade and battle over the link cable. Pokemon Gold and Silver, released in 1999 in Japan, use the MBC3 real time clock so that the game world has day and night and events on certain days of the week. The Pokemon games are credited with reviving sales of the Game Boy late in its life.

Other notable Game Boy games include Super Mario Land in 1989, Super Mario Land 2: 6 Golden Coins in 1992, Wario Land: Super Mario Land 3 in 1994, which introduced Wario as a playable character, The Legend of Zelda: Link's Awakening in 1993, Kirby's Dream Land in 1992, the first Kirby game, made by Masahiro Sakurai at HAL Laboratory, Metroid II: Return of Samus in 1991, Donkey Kong in 1994, which was one of the first games to support the Super Game Boy, Kid Icarus: Of Myths and Monsters, Final Fantasy Legend, Mega Man games, Castlevania: The Adventure, Dr. Mario and Kirby Tilt 'n' Tumble, which has a motion sensor in the cartridge.

The Game Boy competed with the Atari Lynx, released in 1989 with a colour backlit screen but a short battery life and a large case, the Sega Game Gear of 1990, essentially a portable Master System with a colour screen that ran for three to five hours on six AA batteries, and the NEC TurboExpress of 1990, which played TurboGrafx-16 cards on a colour screen. The Game Boy outsold all of them by a wide margin because it was cheaper, lasted longer on batteries and had Tetris and Mario.

The Game Boy and the Game Boy Color together sold 118.69 million units worldwide, making the family one of the best selling video game systems of all time. A Game Boy damaged in a barracks bombing during the Gulf War still worked and was displayed at the Nintendo World store in New York for many years. The Game Boy Advance succeeded the line in 2001 with a 32 bit ARM processor and a wider screen, while remaining compatible with Game Boy and Game Boy Color cartridges. Production of the Game Boy Color ended in 2003.

Homebrew development for the Game Boy continues today. The Game Boy Development Kit, GBDK, is a C compiler toolchain based on SDCC that produces Game Boy ROMs, and many emulators such as SameBoy, BGB, mGBA and Gambatte run the games with high accuracy. Flash cartridges let hobbyists run their own programs on real hardware, and the console's simple, well documented hardware has made it a popular platform for demos, music software and small experiments.
